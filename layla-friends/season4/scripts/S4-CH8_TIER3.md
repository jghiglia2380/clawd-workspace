## THE PIVOT

### Scene 1 (68 words)
Month two of Maplewood Makers had arrived, and the friends gathered in the fort on Saturday morning to examine their progress over the past five weeks. Layla flipped through her notebook with a troubled expression, comparing the numbers she had written each Friday. "Something isn't right," she announced to the group. "We're putting in more effort than ever, but our revenue has stopped growing." The others leaned in, concerned by what they heard.

### Scene 2 (67 words)
Layla directed everyone's attention to the weekly scorecard pinned to the wall. Three consecutive weeks told a frustrating story: ninety-one dollars, ninety-four dollars, ninety-two dollars. The revenue line had gone flat despite longer hours and more deliveries. "We've hit a plateau," Ellis observed, using a word he'd learned from his dad's business books. "Working harder isn't the answer. We need to figure out what's actually going wrong."

### Scene 3 (68 words)
That evening, Layla stayed late at the fort and spread every single order record across the table in careful rows. She separated them into four distinct categories: pottery orders, repair orders, birdhouse orders, and journal orders. Then, with a pencil and fresh paper, she calculated the revenue from each category individually. The sorting took over an hour, but when she finished, she understood exactly where their money was coming from.

### Scene 4 (67 words)
The next morning, Layla created a bar chart on a large piece of paper, drawing four bars of different heights to represent each product line. Pottery towered over everything, generating sixty percent of total revenue. Repairs contributed a solid twenty-five percent. Birdhouses accounted for ten percent, clearly declining. And journals, the product Layla had created and loved, contributed just five percent. She traced that tiny bar with her finger.

### Scene 5 (68 words)
Layla went further, tracking each product's performance across all eight weeks of operation. The trends revealed a clear pattern that was impossible to ignore. Pottery had climbed steadily from forty percent of revenue to sixty percent, gaining momentum every single week. Journals had moved in the opposite direction, declining from twelve percent down to five percent. The last two weeks showed zero journal reorders. The market had spoken clearly.

### Scene 6 (67 words)
At Monday's meeting in the fort, Layla pinned her chart and trend analysis to the wall for everyone to see. "Pottery is our strongest product by far," she said, pointing to the dominant bar. "It generates most of our revenue and it keeps growing." The visual evidence was undeniable. Every friend in the room could see the pattern. Pottery was what their customers valued most.

### Scene 7 (68 words)
"So what does this mean for birdhouses?" asked Ellis, who had been studying the declining trend line. Layla showed him the eight-week trajectory, each week lower than the last. "We should transition birdhouses to order-only," she proposed. "We stop building inventory and only make them when a customer specifically requests one." The team agreed without hesitation. Birdhouses weren't worth the time or materials to produce on speculation anymore.

### Scene 8 (67 words)
Then a heavy silence settled over the fort. Every pair of eyes drifted to the smallest bar on the chart. Journals. Layla's journals. She had conceived and designed them herself back at the very beginning of their business. She selected the paper, designed the binding method, handcrafted every single cover with care and creativity. These journals were her original contribution to Maplewood Makers. And the market had rejected them.

### Scene 9 (68 words)
Riley was the first to speak, and she chose her words with care. "I understand exactly how this feels," she said gently, meeting Layla's eyes across the table. "Remember my morning glory platter from a few weeks ago? I poured everything into it. It was beautiful work. But the customer didn't want morning glories." She paused. "Sometimes what we love creating and what people need to buy aren't the same thing."

### Scene 10 (67 words)
Layla's eyes burned with the effort of holding back tears. "But they're beautiful," she said, her voice barely above a whisper. "I put so many hours into each one." Ellis placed his hand on the table near hers in quiet support. "The data doesn't measure beauty," he said carefully. "It measures what customers choose to spend their money on. And they're choosing pottery, not journals."

### Scene 11 (68 words)
After the meeting ended, Layla walked to Frances's garden alone, needing time away from the fort and her friends. She found Frances methodically trimming thick branches from a thriving rose bush, letting perfectly healthy wood fall to the ground. "Those branches look fine," Layla said, confused by what seemed like destruction. Frances held up her pruning shears. "They are fine," she agreed. "But I'm cutting good branches to make room for great ones."

### Scene 12 (67 words)
Layla settled onto the garden bench, her shoulders heavy with the weight of what she had to decide. "I have to stop making journals," she said. Her voice cracked on the final word. Frances sat beside her and waited patiently. "Pruning is the hardest part of gardening," Frances said softly. "You're removing something alive. Something you planted and grew yourself. But pruning is how the rest grows stronger."

### Scene 13 (68 words)
Monday morning, Layla walked into the workshop with steady determination. She removed every journal-related card from the order board and replaced them with additional pottery time slots. She relocated journal supplies to a storage shelf in the corner. The board looked transformed: cleaner, tighter, more focused. Only pottery and repairs remained active, with a single small note indicating birdhouses were available by special order only. The pivot was real.

### Scene 14 (67 words)
The hours Layla had previously devoted to journals were suddenly available for other work. She had been spending two to three hours each week cutting paper, gluing spines, and stitching covers by hand. That time represented pure opportunity cost that she could now redirect. "Let me help you with pottery preparation," she offered to Riley. "I can mix glazes and wedge clay." Riley's whole face brightened with relief and gratitude.

### Scene 15 (68 words)
With Layla handling material preparation, Riley could dedicate her full attention to the skilled work of shaping and finishing each pottery piece. Her output increased noticeably within the first few days. Bowls, mugs, planters, and small vases emerged from the workshop at a faster pace than ever before. Critically, the quality remained excellent because Riley wasn't being stretched thin anymore. She simply had more focused time for the craft that customers valued.

### Scene 16 (67 words)
When Friday arrived and they gathered around the money jars, the results exceeded their cautious expectations. Revenue had climbed to one hundred and seven dollars, driven entirely by increased pottery output. Meanwhile, costs had dropped to just twenty-eight dollars because they were no longer purchasing journal supplies or stocking birdhouse materials. Profit reached seventy-nine dollars, surpassing their previous best. Only one week into the pivot, and the improvement was measurable.

### Scene 17 (68 words)
The second week post-pivot delivered even stronger results. Revenue reached one hundred and fifteen dollars as word spread about their pottery quality. Costs declined further to twenty-six dollars with the streamlined product focus. Profit hit eighty-nine dollars, a new record that Layla circled twice on the scorecard with a thick marker. The data was confirming what the difficult decision had promised. Cutting away the weakest products strengthened everything that remained.

### Scene 18 (67 words)
Ellis ran through the numbers one final time with characteristic precision. "Revenue trending upward. Costs trending downward. Profit margin at its highest point ever," he summarized. "The data validates the pivot ." Layla nodded slowly, processing the validation alongside the lingering ache of what she had given up. She still missed making journals. Perhaps she always would. But leading meant following the evidence, even when it contradicted personal attachment.

### Scene 19 (68 words)
During lunch that week, Benny shared a piece of neighborhood news that caught everyone's attention. "Neville closed his fix-it shop," he said. "He tried to do everything and couldn't keep the quality up." The contrast landed heavily among the four friends. "We could have ended up the same way," Layla said thoughtfully. "We survived because we were willing to prune. Neville tried to keep every branch, and the whole plant collapsed."

### Scene 20 (67 words)
That evening, the friends gathered around the map in the fort's amber lamplight. The lines that once connected journal customers had faded , dissolved like morning mist. But the pottery lines pulsed with vivid brightness, and fresh new connections were appearing each week. Clusters of pottery customers gathered visibly near the cafe district. Layla traced the glowing lines carefully. "The map reveals what works," she said quietly. "We just needed the courage to follow it."

## FL VOCABULARY

- **pivot** -- a strategic change in business direction based on evidence
- **plateau** -- when growth stops and numbers stay flat despite continued effort
- **opportunity cost** -- the value of what you sacrifice when choosing one option over another
- **pruning** -- deliberately cutting underperforming elements to strengthen what remains
- **revenue decomposition** -- breaking total earnings into product-level categories for analysis
- **market signal** -- patterns in customer behavior that indicate what they actually value
