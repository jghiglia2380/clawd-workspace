# CHAPTER 7: TOO MUCH WORK - ACTIVITY PLAN

**Chapter Theme:** Capacity limits and smart prioritization
**Story Connection:** Based on Scenes 1-20 where Benny overcommits to too many jobs, overwhelming the team and teaching them about saying no, prioritizing tasks, and managing workload realistically
**Target Age:** K-4th Grade (Ages 5-10)
**Activity Type:** Single-session (modular scheduling: daily, weekly, or any frequency)

---

## ACTIVITY TITLE
**"Order Overload: Priority & Delegation Simulation"**

---

## STORY CONNECTION

**Explicit Scene Links:**
- **Scenes 1-3:** Benny accepts jobs without checking capacity, saying yes too fast → Activity begins with rapid-fire task assignment to simulate overcommitment
- **Scenes 4-7:** Team discovers they have 11 jobs but can only handle 6, but decides to try anyway → Activity includes capacity calculation and decision-making under pressure
- **Scenes 8-9:** Everyone rushes, quality suffers, Riley's platter looks "just okay" → Activity demonstrates quality decline when overwhelmed
- **Scenes 10-12:** Mistakes happen (cut board too short, broken birdhouse, late delivery) → Activity tracks errors made under time pressure
- **Scenes 13-17:** Team calculates they earned LESS per hour despite working harder → Activity includes reflection on productivity vs. overwork
- **Scenes 18-20:** Green/red card system helps Benny know when to say no → Activity concludes with creating capacity management tools

---

## LEARNING OBJECTIVES

1. **Financial Literacy:** Understand that working MORE doesn't always mean earning MORE (productivity vs. overwork)
2. **Time Management:** Recognize personal and team capacity limits before committing
3. **Prioritization Skills:** Practice urgent vs. important decision-making under pressure
4. **Delegation:** Learn when and how to divide work among team members
5. **Quality Control:** Experience how rushing decreases quality and increases waste
6. **Communication:** Practice saying "not right now" professionally and offering alternatives

## THREE TIME OPTIONS

### 🕐 20-MINUTE VERSION (Story + Quick Activity)
**Best for:** Tight schedules, younger students (K-1), daily scheduling
**Total time:** 10 min story + 10 min activity = 20 minutes

**Activity:** Solo Task Overload Challenge

**Materials Needed:**
- 5 task cards per student (simple tasks: draw a star, write your name 3 times, count to 20, fold a paper airplane, stack 10 blocks)
- Timer (visible to class)
- Pencils and paper
- Blocks or manipulatives

**Steps:**
1. **Task Assignment (1 min):** Give each student 5 task cards. "You have 3 minutes to complete ALL five tasks. Ready? GO!"
2. **Scramble Time (3 min):** Timer runs. Students frantically try to finish everything. Most won't complete all tasks.
3. **Stop & Count (1 min):** Timer stops. Count: "How many tasks did you finish? Hold up that many fingers."
4. **Reflection (2 min):** "Was that stressful? Did you rush? Did anyone make mistakes?" Point to Benny's story.
5. **What If Discussion (3 min):** "What if you could SPLIT these tasks with a partner? Let's try!"

**Round 2 (if time):** Pairs divide the tasks. Each person does 2-3 tasks. Compare results: more completed, less stress.

**Discussion Questions:**
- "How did it feel to have too many tasks?"
- "Did you finish everything? Why or why not?"
- "Would teamwork have helped?"
- "Like Benny, what happens when we say yes to too much?"

### 🕑 30-MINUTE VERSION (Story + Standard Activity)
**Best for:** Standard class periods, grades 1-3, weekly scheduling
**Total time:** 10 min story + 20 min activity = 30 minutes

**Activity:** Capacity Challenge with Priority Matrix

**Materials Needed:**
- 10 task cards per team (mix of quick/long, important/less important tasks)
- Timer
- Task tracking sheets (checkboxes for each task)
- Red/green capacity cards (like Benny's system)
- Pencils

**Steps:**
1. **Form Teams (1 min):** Groups of 4 students. Assign roles: Manager (assigns tasks), Workers (complete tasks), Timer (tracks time).
2. **Round 1: Overload (7 min):**
   - Give teams 10 task cards with only 7 minutes to complete them
   - Tasks include: build a paper tower, draw a logo, write a price list, fold 5 airplanes, sort 20 items, create a sign, etc.
   - Most teams won't finish. Track what gets completed vs. abandoned.
3. **Debrief Round 1 (3 min):** "How many tasks did your team finish? What got left behind? How did you feel?"
4. **Teach Prioritization (2 min):** Introduce simple priority rule: "Do the MOST IMPORTANT tasks first, even if they're harder."
5. **Round 2: Smart Strategy (5 min):**
   - Same 10 tasks, same 7 minutes
   - This time: teams have 1 minute to PLAN which tasks to prioritize before starting
   - Teams use delegation: each person takes specific tasks
6. **Compare Results (2 min):** "Did more tasks get completed in Round 2? Why? What changed?"

**Discussion Questions:**
- "Which round felt more stressful?"
- "What strategies helped in Round 2?"
- "How is this like what happened to Benny and his team?"
- "What's the difference between being busy and being productive?"
- "When should we say no to new work?"

**Extension:** Create a class "Capacity Card" like Benny's — green on one side (we can take more), red on the other (we're full).

### 🕒 45-MINUTE VERSION (Story + Extended Activity)
**Best for:** Block schedules, grades 3-4, deeper exploration
**Total time:** 10 min story + 35 min activity = 45 minutes

**Activity:** Full Business Overload Simulation with Financial Analysis

**Materials Needed:**
- 15 task cards per team with time estimates and payment values
- Capacity tracking worksheets
- Priority matrix templates (urgent/important grid)
- Quality control rubric sheets
- Play money or point tokens
- Timers for each team
- Red/green capacity indicator cards
- "Client complaint" cards for rushed work

**Steps:**
1. **Business Setup (3 min):**
   - Teams of 4 become "Maplewood Makers" satellite teams
   - Establish baseline capacity: "You can complete 6 high-quality jobs per week"
   - Show payment structure: Quality work = full payment, Rushed work = 50% payment, Late work = customer complaint + 25% payment
2. **The Benny Scenario (2 min):**
   - Teacher role-plays Benny accepting jobs: "I just got us 11 orders! Isn't that great?"
   - Teams must decide: Accept all 11? Or say no to some?
3. **Round 1: Accept Everything (10 min):**
   - Teams try to complete all 11 tasks in 10 minutes (impossible)
   - Each task has quality requirements and time estimates
   - Teams track: tasks completed, quality rating (1-5), time per task, mistakes made
   - Teachers circulate with "quality inspector" role — rushed work gets marked down
4. **Financial Consequences (4 min):**
   - Teams calculate earnings based on quality
   - Example: Completed 9 tasks, but 4 were rushed (half payment), 2 were late (complaint penalty)
   - Calculate earnings per hour of work
   - Compare to "if we'd done 6 tasks well" scenario
5. **Priority Matrix Training (3 min):**
   - Teach urgent/important grid:
     - Urgent + Important = Do NOW
     - Important but Not Urgent = Schedule carefully
     - Urgent but Not Important = Delegate or quick handle
     - Neither = Say no or do last
   - Sort the 11 tasks into the grid together
6. **Round 2: Strategic Approach (8 min):**
   - Same 11 tasks offered
   - Teams have 2 minutes to:
     - Use priority matrix to select 6-7 tasks
     - Delegate tasks to team members based on skills
     - Create "waiting list" for tasks they can't do now
   - Execute with focus on quality over quantity
   - Track completion, quality, earnings
7. **Financial Comparison & Reflection (5 min):**
   - Teams calculate Round 2 earnings
   - Compare Round 1 vs Round 2:
     - Tasks completed
     - Quality ratings
     - Total earnings
     - Earnings per hour
     - Stress level (self-report 1-10)
   - Create comparison chart on board

**Discussion Questions:**
- "Which round made more money? Why?"
- "How did the priority matrix help you decide what to do?"
- "What happened to quality when you rushed?"
- "If you were Benny, how would you tell Mrs. Garcia 'not right now'?"
- "What's the hidden cost of saying yes to everything?" (stress, mistakes, reputation, wasted materials)
- "How can you tell when you're at capacity?"
- "What system could you use in real life to avoid overcommitting?" (calendar, task list, capacity card)

**Extension Ideas:**
- Create personal capacity cards (green/yellow/red) for homework management
- Role-play scenarios: "Someone asks for help but you're full — what do you say?"
- Calculate real-world "earnings per hour" for various scenarios
- Research how real businesses manage capacity (waitlists, appointment systems, seasonal closures)
- Write a "Business Policy" for accepting new work: criteria and limits

## FL INTEGRATION

**Chapter 7 Financial Literacy Concepts:**

1. **Capacity as a Business Constraint**
   - Core Lesson: Every business has limits — time, people, materials. Operating beyond capacity decreases quality and profit
   - Activity Connection: Students experience the mathematical reality that 11 tasks > 6 capacity = failure
   - Age-Appropriate: K-1 feel overwhelm; 3-4 calculate earnings per hour and see financial impact

2. **Productivity vs. Overwork**
   - Core Lesson: Working harder/longer doesn't always mean earning more — quality and efficiency matter more than quantity
   - Activity Connection: Teams discover they earn MORE per hour doing FEWER tasks well than MORE tasks badly
   - Age-Appropriate: Concrete experience through comparison of Round 1 vs. Round 2 earnings

3. **Hidden Costs of Rushing**
   - Core Lesson: Rushing creates waste (mistakes, rework, damaged materials, customer complaints) that eats into profit
   - Activity Connection: Rushed work receives lower payment; mistakes require starting over; late delivery causes complaints
   - Age-Appropriate: Scaffolded from simple quality ratings to full cost analysis with waste calculation

4. **Strategic "No" is Good Business**
   - Core Lesson: Saying no to work you can't handle well protects your reputation, team health, and profit margins
   - Activity Connection: Teams learn to use capacity indicators and waitlists instead of accepting everything
   - Age-Appropriate: From simple yes/no practice to professional alternatives ("not right now, but next week")

**Non-Moralistic Approach:**
- Activity presents capacity management as strategy, not character flaw
- No shame language: "Benny was bad for saying yes" ❌ Instead: "Benny learned his capacity limit" ✓
- Acknowledges desire to help people is GOOD — the lesson is about METHOD, not motive
- Focuses on systems and tools (capacity cards, priority matrices) rather than willpower
- Celebrates learning from mistakes rather than punishing them
- Recognizes that overwork culture exists — this teaches kids to resist it early

## TEACHER TIPS

**Preparation:**
- Create task cards ahead of time with variety (quick/long, easy/hard, important/less important)
- Decide whether to use play money or point system (younger kids: points; older kids: money)
- Set up visual timer where all teams can see it
- Prepare capacity tracking worksheets (or template on board for copying)
- Have backup tasks ready if teams finish faster than expected
- Consider noise level — this activity gets energetic! Set volume expectations ahead of time

**Classroom Management:**
- Establish "Pause" signal (raised hand, bell, lights off) to stop activity for announcements
- Set clear rules: no throwing task cards, no arguing over who does what, manager's decision is final
- Circulate during both rounds with "quality inspector" clipboard — check work and give feedback
- Prepare for: teams that give up in Round 1 (encourage them to do their best even if they can't finish), teams that rush through everything without reading instructions, conflict over task delegation
- Celebrate mistakes as learning opportunities ("Look what we discovered about rushing!")

**Common Challenges & Solutions:**

| Challenge | Solution |
|-----------|----------|
| "We finished all 10 tasks in Round 1!" (unlikely but possible) | They either have high capacity OR rushed and didn't meet quality standards — check their work carefully |
| "This is impossible!" (frustration in Round 1) | "You're feeling what Benny's team felt! Let's see if strategy helps in Round 2." |
| "I don't want to be the manager!" (role resistance) | Rotate roles every few minutes, or allow students to pick roles based on comfort |
| Team members not delegating evenly | Pause and model: "Manager, assign one task to each person. Everyone gets one task first, then we'll see if there's time for more." |
| Students rushing through tasks without quality | That's the POINT — let it happen in Round 1, then address in debrief: "What happened to quality when you rushed?" |
| Arguments over priority | Teach consensus: "If you disagree, do the task everyone agrees is important first." Or use manager as tie-breaker. |
| "We want to do Round 2 first!" (after hearing instructions) | "In real life, Benny didn't get a practice round. You're learning from his mistake, just like he did!" |
| Younger students overwhelmed by activity pace | Reduce task count, extend time limits, allow teacher support during Round 1 |

**Time Management:**
- Round 1 intentionally creates chaos — don't intervene too much, let them struggle (safely)
- Build in transition time between rounds for cleanup and regrouping
- If running long, skip financial calculation in 20-min version and just discuss feelings
- Have extension ready for teams that finish Round 2 early: "Create a 'How to Prioritize' poster for younger students"

**Differentiation in Practice:**
- Younger students: Use picture task cards, reduce task count, extend time
- Older students: Add complexity (tasks with dependencies, surprise new tasks mid-round)
- Mixed-ability teams: Assign roles strategically (strong reader = manager, organized student = tracker)
- Students who get anxious: Give them the tracker or timer role (important but less pressure)
- Advanced students: Challenge them to design a NEW capacity management system better than the green/red card

**Making It Real:**
- Connect to students' lives: "Have you ever had too much homework? Too many activities? How did it feel?"
- Share teacher experiences: "I sometimes overcommit too! Here's what I do..."
- Use real business examples: "Restaurants have limited tables — when they're full, you make a reservation for later"
- Link to season story: "Remember how Benny felt when customers were unhappy? That's the real cost of overcommitting."

**Family Extension Ideas:**

1. **Family Capacity Calendar:** Create a visual family calendar showing when everyone is "green" (available) or "red" (full). Practice saying no to activities when the family is at capacity.

## SAFETY NOTES

**Physical Safety:**
- Monitor energy level during timed rounds — some students may literally run around; set movement boundaries
- Ensure task activities are age-appropriate and safe (no sharp scissors for K-1, safe stacking materials)
- Watch for students standing on chairs to stack items — provide stable platforms if needed
- Clear workspace of tripping hazards when students are moving quickly

**Emotional Safety:**
- **Overwhelm is the Point:** Round 1 is designed to create (safe) stress. Monitor for students who become genuinely distressed vs. productively challenged.
- **Normalize Struggle:** Frame Round 1 failure as expected: "Nobody is supposed to finish everything — that's the lesson!"
- **Celebrate Learning from Mistakes:** Emphasize that Benny made a mistake AND learned from it. Mistakes are learning opportunities, not failures.
- **Opt-Out Option:** Students who are overwhelmed can take the "observer/consultant" role and advise teams instead of participating directly
- **No Shame for Capacity Limits:** Some students can handle more than others — that's okay! Personal capacity varies.
- **Team Conflict:** Monitor delegation discussions for fairness and respect. Intervene if one student is dominating or excluding others.

**General:**
- Set clear expectations: this activity will feel chaotic in Round 1 — that's intentional
- Provide water break between rounds if needed (stress is real!)
- Have quiet reflection space available for students who need sensory break
- Debrief thoroughly — don't end on the stress of Round 1 without the resolution of Round 2 and reflection

## COMMON CHALLENGES

| Challenge | Why It Happens | Solution |
|-----------|---------------|----------|
| "Round 1 is too easy!" (team finishes all tasks) | Tasks too simple OR team rushed without quality | Quality check! Review their work against rubric — did they meet standards? If yes, celebrate and add bonus challenge tasks. If rushed, mark down and discuss. |
| "This isn't fair!" (frustration in Round 1) | Overwhelm leads to complaints of unfairness | "You're right — 11 tasks in 7 minutes ISN'T fair to you. That's exactly what happened to Benny's team! Let's see if we can make Round 2 more fair through strategy." |
| One student dominates task assignment | Personality/leadership dynamics | Rotate manager role every 3 minutes, or teach consensus delegation: "Everyone picks one task they're good at, then manager assigns the rest." |
| Team gives up mid-Round 1 | Overwhelm → shutdown | Encourage: "Do as many as you can! Every task you finish counts." Normalize: "You're supposed to feel overwhelmed — that's the lesson Benny learned too." |
| Arguments over which tasks are "important" | Different perspectives on priority | Teach: "If you disagree, ask: Which one will customers notice most? Which one makes the most money? Which one is due soonest?" Or: manager makes final call. |
| Students ignore time limits and keep working | Excitement/task completion drive | Clear stop signal (bell) and enforce strictly: "Real businesses have deadlines — when time is up, time is UP." |
| "We want to skip Round 1 now that we know it's bad" | Students resist uncomfortable learning | "Benny didn't get to skip the hard part. He learned by trying and struggling. You'll learn more by experiencing both rounds." |
| Younger students near tears from stress | Overwhelm too intense for developmental level | Shorten Round 1, reduce task count, provide more support. Frame as game: "Let's see how many we CAN do!" not "You must do all of them." |
