# CHAPTER 4: THE UNHAPPY CUSTOMER - ACTIVITY PLAN

**Chapter Theme:** Customer satisfaction and quality
**Story Connection:** Based on Scenes 1-20 where Riley delivers mugs that Mrs. Patterson doesn't like, learning to handle unhappy customers with grace and that getting it right matters more than being right
**Target Age:** K-4th Grade (Ages 5-10)
**Activity Type:** Single-session (modular scheduling: daily, weekly, or any frequency)

---

## ACTIVITY TITLE
**"Customer Service Role-Play Challenge"**

---

## STORY CONNECTION

**Explicit Scene Links:**
- **Scenes 1-3:** Mrs. Patterson orders a plate with flowers, Riley chooses blue morning glories → Activity includes taking customer orders and clarifying details
- **Scenes 4-7:** Customer sends plate back — it's beautiful but wrong → Activity demonstrates that quality isn't enough if it doesn't match expectations
- **Scenes 8-9:** Team calculates cost of redoing work (lost profit) → Activity includes calculating the financial cost of mistakes
- **Scenes 10-11:** Riley remakes the plate the right way, following customer specifications → Activity practices redoing work correctly after complaints
- **Scenes 12-13:** Ellis proactively fixes his birdhouse before delivering → Activity includes quality control and preventing problems
- **Scenes 14-16:** Team creates "show a drawing first" rule to prevent future mix-ups → Activity concludes with creating customer service policies
- **Scenes 17-19:** Riley sells the "wrong" plate as "Artist's Choice" to someone who loves blue → Activity explores finding the right customer for rejected work
- **Scene 20:** Map shows that fixing problems builds reputation and trust → Activity reflection on how good service leads to repeat business

---

## LEARNING OBJECTIVES

1. **Financial Literacy:** Understand that poor customer service costs money (refunds, remakes, lost business)
2. **Communication Skills:** Learn to ask clarifying questions before starting work
3. **Problem-Solving:** Practice staying calm and finding solutions when customers are unhappy
4. **Quality Control:** Understand that meeting specifications matters as much as doing quality work
5. **Reputation Management:** Grasp that how you handle complaints affects future business
6. **Empathy:** Develop perspective-taking skills (customer's view vs. creator's view)

## THREE TIME OPTIONS

### 🕐 20-MINUTE VERSION (Story + Quick Activity)
**Best for:** Tight schedules, younger students (K-1), daily scheduling
**Total time:** 10 min story + 10 min activity = 20 minutes

**Activity:** Customer Service Response Practice

**Materials Needed:**
- 3 pre-written scenario cards
- Paper for writing responses
- Pencils or crayons

**Steps:**
1. **Introduction & Scenario Reading (2 min):** Teacher reads aloud: "A customer says your drawing has the wrong colors. What do you say?"
2. **Partner Discussion (3 min):** Turn to partner. Decide together: What's the BEST thing to say? What's the WORST thing to say?
3. **Share & Compare (3 min):** Pairs share their "best response." Teacher writes top 3 responses on board.
4. **Second Scenario (2 min):** Quick repeat with new scenario. Faster decision-making.

**Discussion Questions:**
- "How would YOU feel if you ordered something and it was wrong?"
- "What words make an unhappy customer feel better?"
- "Is it better to argue or to fix the problem?"

---

### 🕑 30-MINUTE VERSION (Story + Standard Activity)
**Best for:** Standard class periods, grades 1-3, weekly scheduling
**Total time:** 10 min story + 20 min activity = 30 minutes

**Activity:** Role-Play Customer Complaint Scenarios

**Materials Needed:**
- 6 scenario cards (3 complaint types)
- "Customer" and "Business Owner" role badges or name tags
- Response tracker sheets
- Pencils

**Steps:**
1. **Model Good vs. Bad Responses (4 min):** Teacher demonstrates WRONG way (arguing, blaming) vs. RIGHT way (listening, apologizing, solving). Exaggerate for humor and clarity.
2. **Partner Role Assignment (1 min):** Pairs choose who's Customer first, who's Business Owner first.
3. **Round 1 Role-Play (5 min):** Customer reads their complaint from card. Business Owner responds. Aim for: Listen, Apologize, Solve.
4. **Switch & Round 2 (5 min):** Partners swap roles. New scenario card. Repeat.
5. **Class Debrief (3 min):** "What responses worked best? Which ones made the customer feel heard?"
6. **Response Tracker (2 min):** Students check off on worksheet: "I listened ☐ I apologized ☐ I offered a solution ☐"

**Discussion Questions:**
- "Was it hard to stay calm when the customer complained?"
- "How did it feel to BE the unhappy customer?"
- "What's the difference between 'I'm sorry' and 'It's not my fault'?"
- "Would you shop there again if they fixed your problem nicely?"
- "What if the customer is WRONG but still upset?"

**Extension:** Create a class "Customer Service Rules" poster with student-generated ideas.

---

### 🕒 45-MINUTE VERSION (Story + Extended Activity)
**Best for:** Block schedules, grades 3-4, deeper exploration
**Total time:** 10 min story + 35 min activity = 45 minutes

**Activity:** Complete Customer Service Training with Cost Analysis

**Materials Needed:**
- 8+ detailed scenario cards (varying difficulty)
- Role badges (Customer, Business Owner, Observer)
- Customer Service Response Rubric (printed)
- Cost calculation worksheets
- "Lost Customer" impact cards
- Certificates for "Excellent Service" awards

**Steps:**
1. **Training Introduction & Cost of Complaints (5 min):**
   - Discuss: "Riley's mistake cost them $16 in materials and only $2 profit left. Let's calculate costs of bad service."
   - Mini-lesson: One unhappy customer tells 10 people. Show visual of 1 person becoming 10 lost customers.
2. **Demonstrate Response Strategy (4 min):**
   - Teacher models the 3 L's: LISTEN (don't interrupt), LEARN (ask questions to understand), LOVE (fix it with care)
   - Show both defensive response and excellent response side-by-side
3. **Scenario Round 1: Basic Complaints (6 min):**
   - Groups of 3: Customer, Business Owner, Observer
   - Observer uses rubric to score response (Did they listen? Apologize? Solve?)
   - 2 minutes per scenario, then switch roles
4. **Scenario Round 2: Harder Situations (6 min):**
   - Increase difficulty (angry customer, customer asking for refund, customer wants free replacement)
   - Same rotation, new roles for each
5. **Cost Calculation Exercise (6 min):**
   - Worksheet: "The plate cost $8 to make. Riley remade it ($8 more). Profit went from $10 to $2. How much did the mistake cost?"
   - Calculate: "If an unhappy customer tells 10 people and each would have spent $20, how much future business did you lose?" ($200)
   - Students complete 3-4 cost scenarios
6. **Creating Service Policies (5 min):**
   - Small groups write 2-3 "business rules" to prevent complaints (like Riley's "show a drawing first" rule)
   - Examples: "Ask 3 questions before starting work," "Let customer approve samples," "Check finished work before delivering"
7. **Award Ceremony & Reflection (3 min):**
   - Give "Excellent Service" certificates to students who scored highest on Observer rubrics
   - Class votes: "Best problem-solver," "Calmest under pressure," "Best listener"

**Discussion Questions:**
- "Why did Riley's beautiful plate get rejected even though it was good quality?"
- "What questions should Riley have asked BEFORE painting the plate?"
- "How much did the mistake cost? Just the $16 in materials, or more?"
- "Ellis fixed his birdhouse before the customer even noticed. Why was that smart?"
- "What does 'the customer is always right' mean? Are they REALLY always right?"
- "How can one unhappy customer hurt your business even if you fix their problem?"
- "What's the difference between a refund, a redo, and a discount?"
- "If you handle a complaint really well, can that customer become MORE loyal than before?"

**Extension Ideas:**
- Write "apology letters" for different complaint scenarios
- Research real company customer service policies (Zappos, Amazon)
- Create a customer service training video for younger students
- Track: How many complaints does your favorite store/restaurant handle? Interview staff.
- Design a "Customer Satisfaction Survey" for a real or imaginary business
- Role-play increasingly difficult scenarios (screaming customer, customer who's clearly wrong, customer asking for unreasonable demands)

## DIFFERENTIATION NOTES

**By Reading Level (Tier):**

**Tier 1 (Levels A-D / K-1):**
- Very simple scenarios: "The toy is broken. What do you say?"
- Focus on polite language: "I'm sorry. I will fix it."
- Teacher heavily models responses before student practice
- Use pictures to show facial expressions (angry face → happy face after good service)
- Basic vocabulary: sorry, fix, help, listen

**Tier 2 (Levels E-J / 1st-2nd):**
- Slightly complex scenarios: "The customer wanted red but you gave them blue."
- Introduce the 3-step response: Listen → Apologize → Fix
- Students practice with sentence frames: "I hear that you ___. I'm sorry. I can ___."
- Vocabulary expansion: apologize, solution, mistake, disappointed, satisfied

**Tier 3 (Levels K-M / 2nd Grade):**
- Multi-step scenarios requiring questions: "The customer says it's wrong but won't say why."
- Add clarifying questions: "Can you tell me more about what you expected?"
- Discuss difference between refund, redo, and discount
- Vocabulary: refund, replacement, clarify, expectation, quality control

**Tier 4 (Levels N-O-P / 3rd-4th):**
- Complex, emotionally charged scenarios: "The customer is yelling and demanding a refund."
- Practice de-escalation techniques (staying calm, validating feelings, offering choices)
- Cost analysis: Calculate financial impact of complaints and lost customers
- Write formal apology letters and service recovery plans
- Advanced vocabulary: de-escalate, compensation, reputation management, customer retention, service recovery

**For English Language Learners:**
- Visual scenario cards with pictures showing the problem
- Bilingual vocabulary cards: sorry/lo siento, fix/arreglar, mistake/error, customer/cliente
- Sentence frames for responses: "I am sorry that ___. I will ___ to fix it."
- Partner with fluent speaker for role-play support
- Use gestures and tone modeling (calm voice, open body language)

**For Students with Learning Differences:**
- Simplified scenarios with one clear problem and one clear solution
- Visual cue cards showing response steps (1. Listen, 2. Say sorry, 3. Fix it)
- Option to observe first round before participating
- Provide script for students who need structured language support
- Use puppets or stuffed animals instead of direct role-play if student is anxious
- Quiet practice space for one-on-one role-play before group participation

**For Advanced Students:**
- Lead demonstration role-plays for the class
- Create NEW scenario cards based on real-life experiences
- Act as "Observer" with detailed rubric, providing written feedback
- Research real company customer service fails and successes (present to class)
- Write a "Customer Service Training Manual" for younger students
- Design a flowchart: "What to do when a customer complains"

## TEACHER TIPS

**Preparation:**
- Pre-write scenario cards at varying difficulty levels (start easy, build to harder)
- Practice modeling the WRONG response yourself (make it funny/exaggerated so students see the contrast)
- Prepare cost calculation examples using simple numbers for younger students
- Have backup roles for odd-numbered classes (Observer role works well)
- Consider which students might struggle with criticism and pair them supportively

**Classroom Management:**
- Set clear boundaries: Role-play should never involve real insults or hurtful words
- Use signal (bell, hand raise) to pause role-plays if they get too heated
- Remind students: "This is practice. We're all learning. No one is really in trouble."
- Celebrate students who handle the "customer" role well — being angry without being mean is a skill
- Have a "cool-down" plan for students who get genuinely upset during role-play (step out, observe instead)

**Common Challenges & Solutions:**

| Challenge | Solution |
|-----------|----------|
| "I don't want to be the mean customer!" | Reframe: "You're ACTING, like in a movie. It helps your partner learn. You're being helpful!" |
| Student gets defensive during role-play | Pause. Remind: "This isn't real. Let's try again with the calm voice we practiced." |
| "The customer is being TOTALLY unfair!" | Perfect teaching moment: "Yes. Sometimes customers are wrong. But you still have to be polite. Let's practice." |
| Student freezes and can't think of response | Provide sentence frame on a card: "I hear that ___. I'm sorry. I can ___." |
| Role-play feels boring/low energy | Add "audience" — class watches and votes on best response. Adds performance energy. |
| Students rush through without listening | Add Observer role with checklist: "Did they let customer finish talking?" Peer accountability. |
| Giggling/silliness during serious scenarios | Channel it: "This IS funny because we're practicing! But let's also see if you can solve it for real." |

**Time Management:**
- Role-plays always take longer than expected — build in buffer time
- Use timer for each role-play round (2-3 min max per scenario keeps energy high)
- If running short on time, do whole-class role-play instead of pairs (teacher plays customer, volunteer responds)
- Extension students can write new scenarios while others finish
- Have early-finishers observe and score ongoing role-plays using rubrics

**Differentiation in Practice:**
- Pair anxious students with supportive partners for first round
- Let students who struggle with social interaction use puppets or stuffed animals for role-play
- Give advanced students the "difficult customer" scenarios while simpler ones go to developing students
- Provide visual cue cards (step-by-step response guide) for students who need scaffolding
- Allow students to opt-out of performing and observe/score instead if genuinely uncomfortable

## SAFETY NOTES

**Emotional Safety:**
- **Monitor Role-Plays:** Ensure "angry customer" doesn't become actual bullying or hurtful language
- **Safe Words:** Establish that anyone can say "pause" if role-play feels too real or upsetting
- **Debrief After:** Remind students after intense role-plays: "That was pretend. Everyone here is kind and respectful."
- **Opt-Out Option:** Students genuinely uncomfortable with confrontation can observe and score instead of performing
- **Model Respect:** Teacher should participate in role-plays to model appropriate "angry but not mean" tone

**Psychological Safety:**
- **Normalize Mistakes:** Emphasize that EVERYONE makes mistakes in business; the lesson is how you respond
- **No Shaming:** Never use a student's real mistake as a scenario (keeps examples fictional or from story only)
- **Celebrate Effort:** Recognize students who struggle with criticism but try the role-play anyway
- **Private Feedback:** If a student responds poorly in role-play, coach them privately rather than correcting in front of peers

**General:**
- Keep role-play volume at conversational level (no actual yelling/screaming)
- Clear expectations: "We can ACT frustrated, but we stay respectful"
- If students have real conflicts with each other, don't pair them for role-play
- Watch for students getting genuinely upset and offer breaks as needed

- ✅ Can differentiate between situations requiring refund vs. redo vs. explanation
- ✅ Shows leadership by modeling excellent service for peers

## COMMON CHALLENGES

| Challenge | Why It Happens | Solution |
|-----------|---------------|----------|
| Student takes "angry customer" too far (mean/hurtful) | Doesn't understand difference between acting frustrated and being cruel | Pause role-play. Model: "Frustrated voice vs. mean voice. Let's try frustrated." Clear boundary: no name-calling or insults even in role-play. |
| Student gets defensive: "But I DID do it right!" | Natural response to criticism; hasn't learned to separate self-worth from work quality | Acknowledge feeling: "It DOES feel bad to be criticized. But in customer service, we focus on fixing, not defending. Let's try: 'I hear you. Let me make it right.'" |
| Giggles during serious scenarios | Nervous energy or immaturity | Redirect: "It IS funny because we're practicing! But imagine this was YOUR business and you'd lose money. Let's try once more seriously." |
| Student freezes when faced with complaint | Anxiety or lack of confidence | Provide sentence frames on cards. Let them observe first round. Pair with supportive partner. |
| "The customer is being totally unfair and wrong!" | Sees injustice and wants to argue for what's "right" | Great teaching moment: "You're RIGHT. The customer IS wrong. But do you want to be right, or do you want their money and good review? Business sometimes means choosing success over being right." |
| Role-play feels fake/wooden | Students not emotionally engaged | Add stakes: "You NEED this customer to be happy or your business goes bankrupt. Make them believe you care." Add audience voting for "most convincing." |
| Over-apologizing or excessive people-pleasing | Some students overly compliant or anxious to please | Balance lesson: "You apologize for YOUR mistake, but you don't have to say sorry for things that aren't your fault. Let's practice: 'I understand you're disappointed. Here's what I can do.'" |

## EXTENSIONS & VARIATIONS

**For Early Finishers:**
1. Write 2-3 NEW scenario cards based on businesses they know
2. Create a "Customer Service Champions" poster highlighting best responses
3. Design a flowchart: "What to Do When a Customer Complains"
4. Observe ongoing role-plays and score using rubrics
5. Practice writing a formal apology letter for one scenario

**For Advanced Learners:**
1. Research real company customer service failures (United Airlines guitar, Comcast call recording) and successes (Zappos overnight shipping story). Present to class.
2. Create a comprehensive "Customer Service Training Manual" for younger students with illustrations
3. Write and perform a customer service training skit for another class
4. Design a customer satisfaction survey for a real or imaginary business
5. Calculate complex scenarios: "If you have 100 customers and 10 complain, and each tells 5 people, and you lose 30% of those potential customers, how many total customers did you lose?"
6. Interview a local business owner: "What's the hardest customer complaint you've handled?"

**For Additional Days/Sessions:**
- **Day 2:** Practice writing professional apology letters or emails
- **Day 3:** Create and present service policy manuals for different business types
- **Week 2:** Bring in real customer service scenarios from news or family experiences
- **Ongoing:** Use "customer service check-in" as classroom management tool (how can we improve our service to each other?)

**Seasonal Variations:**
- **Back-to-School:** Complaints about school supplies or cafeteria food
- **Holidays:** Complaints about gifts, decorations, or holiday service delays
- **Spring:** Complaints about outdoor services (landscaping, car wash)
- **Any Season:** Tailor scenarios to current classroom interests (sports, popular toys, trending topics)

## CHARACTER DEVELOPMENT ALIGNMENT

**Riley (Creative/Emotional):**
- Story shows Riley learning the hard lesson that beautiful work means nothing if it's not what the customer wanted
- Students experience Riley's emotional journey: pride → hurt → frustration → acceptance → growth
- Activity lets students practice what Riley learned: ask first, deliver what's promised, fix mistakes gracefully
- Reinforces that creativity matters, but so does listening to others' needs

**Layla (Leader/Organizer):**
- Story shows Layla calculating costs and creating systems to prevent future problems
- Students use Layla's analytical approach: tracking costs, creating policies, documenting solutions
- Activity includes Layla's "show a drawing first" rule as a model for student-created policies
- Reinforces that good leaders learn from mistakes and build systems to prevent them

**Ellis (Analytical/Detail-Oriented):**
- Story shows Ellis catching and fixing his own mistake BEFORE the customer sees it (proactive quality control)
- Students learn Ellis's preventative approach: check your work, fix problems early, build reputation through reliability
- Activity includes Ellis's example as "best practice" customer service
- Reinforces that quality control saves money and builds trust

**Benny (Enthusiastic/Social):**
- Story shows Benny delivering products and reporting customer feedback (even when it's hard to share bad news)
- Students practice Benny's role: being the messenger, communicating clearly, staying positive under pressure
- Activity includes Benny's communication skills in role-play scenarios
- Reinforces that honest communication, even about problems, helps the team improve
