# CHAPTER 5: THE COMPETITION - ACTIVITY PLAN

**Chapter Theme:** Competing through differentiation, not price wars
**Story Connection:** Based on Scenes 1-20 where friends face competition from Neville who charges less, but they discover quality and service matter more than being the cheapest
**Target Age:** K-4th Grade (Ages 5-10)
**Activity Type:** Single-session (modular scheduling: daily, weekly, or any frequency)

---

## ACTIVITY TITLE
**"Competing Businesses: Differentiation Game"**

---

## STORY CONNECTION

**Explicit Scene Links:**
- **Scenes 1-3:** Friends learn a competitor (Neville) is charging less → Activity introduces competing businesses selling similar products
- **Scenes 4-6:** Ellis discovers Neville's lower prices and feels pressure to match them → Activity Round 1 demonstrates price war dynamics
- **Scenes 7-8:** Layla shows that charging too little eliminates profit → Activity includes profit calculation revealing the limits of price cutting
- **Scenes 9-11:** Mr. Mason advises finding what makes them special; they raise prices instead of lowering them → Activity Round 2 focuses on differentiation strategies
- **Scenes 12-17:** They add guarantees and quality service; cheap work fails while quality wins → Activity demonstrates how differentiation attracts customers willing to pay more
- **Scenes 18-20:** Trust and good work build their reputation stronger than competing on price → Activity concludes with reflection on competitive advantage

---

## LEARNING OBJECTIVES

1. **Financial Literacy:** Understand that competing on price alone (race to the bottom) hurts everyone
2. **Business Strategy:** Learn that differentiation (being unique) is smarter than price wars
3. **Value Proposition:** Practice identifying and communicating what makes a business special
4. **Economics Concepts:** Grasp competitive advantage and market positioning
5. **Critical Thinking:** Evaluate short-term vs. long-term business strategies
6. **Problem-Solving:** Discover creative ways to stand out in a crowded market

## THREE TIME OPTIONS

### 🕐 20-MINUTE VERSION (Story + Quick Activity)
**Best for:** Tight schedules, younger students (K-1), daily scheduling
**Total time:** 10 min story + 10 min activity = 20 minutes

**Activity:** Two Stores Competition Simulation

**Materials Needed:**
- 2 "stores" (desks or tables with signs: Store A and Store B)
- Simple products to "sell" (paper bookmarks, stickers, or drawings)
- Play money (paper cutouts)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for tracking

**Steps:**
1. **Setup (2 min):** Create two identical stores selling the same bookmark for $2. Each has 5 customers (students with $5 play money each).
2. **Round 1: Price War (3 min):** Store A drops price to $1.50. Most customers go there. Store B drops to $1. Customers switch. Store A drops to 75¢. Race to the bottom — neither store makes money.
3. **Round 2: Differentiation (3 min):** Reset. This time Store A stays at $2 but adds a "special design" or "free sticker with purchase." Store B stays at $2 but offers "personalized name writing." Customers split between stores based on preference, not price.
4. **Discussion (2 min):** "Which round was better for the stores? Why? What happened when stores just kept lowering prices?"

**Discussion Questions:**
- "In Round 1, why did both stores end up making almost no money?"
- "In Round 2, did price matter as much? What else mattered?"
- "Would you rather buy the cheapest thing or something special that costs a little more?"

### 🕑 30-MINUTE VERSION (Story + Standard Activity)
**Best for:** Standard class periods, grades 1-3, weekly scheduling
**Total time:** 10 min story + 20 min activity = 30 minutes

**Activity:** Four Businesses Differentiation Challenge

**Materials Needed:**
- 4 team stations (desks or tables)
- Index cards for "business cards"
- Play money for customers
- Chart paper for tracking sales
- Markers, colored pencils
- Product samples (can be imaginary: paper menus, drawn items)

**Steps:**
1. **Setup Teams (3 min):** Divide class into 4 businesses. Each sells the same product (lemonade, bookmarks, custom drawings, etc.). Give each team $20 play money as starting capital.
2. **Round 1: Price War (5 min):** All teams set prices. Customers (teacher or rotating students) shop at cheapest store. Prices drop. Track each team's revenue on board. Calculate profit (revenue - $6 cost per item). Teams realize competing on price alone leads to razor-thin margins.
3. **Strategy Huddle (3 min):** Teacher pause. Discuss: "Is there a better way to compete than just being cheapest?" Introduce concept: What makes your business SPECIAL? (Quality, service, speed, customization, guarantees, personality)
4. **Round 2: Differentiation (7 min):** Each team must add ONE thing that makes them unique (NOT price). Examples: "We customize your order," "We guarantee it or your money back," "Fastest service in town," "Eco-friendly materials." Create signs advertising their differentiation. Customers now shop based on what they value, not just price.
5. **Reflection (2 min):** Count final profits. Discuss: "Did adding something special help you charge more? Which businesses made the most profit? Why?"

**Discussion Questions:**
- "What happened to profit in Round 1 when everyone kept lowering prices?"
- "What differentiations did teams add in Round 2?"
- "Would you pay more for something if it came with a guarantee or was made just for you?"
- "How is this like the story where Neville's cheap shelf fell, but Ellis's quality work lasted?"
- "What does 'competitive advantage' mean?"

**Extension:** Create a class "Business Advice Poster": "When you face competition, don't just lower prices. Find what makes you special!"

### 🕒 45-MINUTE VERSION (Story + Extended Activity)
**Best for:** Block schedules, grades 3-4, deeper exploration
**Total time:** 10 min story + 35 min activity = 45 minutes

**Activity:** Complete Market Competition with Analysis

**Materials Needed:**
- 4 team stations with signage materials
- Detailed business planning sheets (templates)
- Play money (customers get $10 each)
- Product materials (craft supplies, printed "goods," or menus)
- Chart paper for data tracking and graphing
- Customer feedback cards
- Differentiation strategy worksheets

**Steps:**
1. **Business Setup (5 min):**
   - Form 4 competing businesses
   - Each sells similar product (custom greeting cards, decorated bookmarks, mini art prints)
   - Each receives: $30 starting capital, materials cost sheet ($4/item to make), business planning worksheet
   - Teams name their business and create basic signage
2. **Round 1: Price Competition (8 min):**
   - Teams set initial prices ($5-10 range suggested)
   - "Customers" (students rotate, or invite another class) shop with play money
   - Prices inevitably drop as teams compete
   - Track: each team's prices, number of items sold, revenue, costs, profit
   - Calculate profit margin: (Revenue - Costs) / Revenue × 100
3. **Analysis Break (4 min):**
   - Teacher facilitates data review: "Business A sold 8 items at $3 each = $24 revenue, but costs were $32 (8×$4). They LOST $8!"
   - Graph profit margins on board
   - Discuss: "What went wrong? Can you stay in business selling for less than it costs to make?"
   - Introduce concept: "Race to the bottom" — when businesses compete only on price, everyone loses
4. **Differentiation Strategy Session (5 min):**
   - Teach framework: Competitive Advantage = What Makes You Special
   - Options: Quality (better materials, craftsmanship), Service (customization, speed, guarantees), Brand (personality, story, values), Innovation (unique features)
   - Each team completes Differentiation Worksheet:
     * What will make us different?
     * Why would customers pay more for this?
     * What's our new slogan/promise?
   - Teams redesign their business with new positioning (NOT price-based)
5. **Round 2: Differentiation Competition (8 min):**
   - Teams reopen with new strategies
   - Examples seen in practice: "Custom names on every order," "100% recycled materials," "30-second guarantee or it's free," "Any color you want," "Includes matching envelope," "Artist signature on back"
   - Customers shop again (reset play money)
   - Track sales, revenue, profit
   - Customer feedback: "Why did you choose this business?"
6. **Post-Market Analysis (5 min):**
   - Compare Round 1 vs Round 2 profits
   - Graph results: Price vs. Profit Margin
   - Identify which differentiation strategies worked best
   - Survey customers: "What made you willing to pay more?"
   - Calculate: Did differentiation allow higher prices AND better margins?
   - Create class findings poster: "Differentiation beats price wars"

**Discussion Questions:**
- "In Round 1, what happened to businesses that just kept lowering prices?"
- "Give an example of a differentiation strategy a team used."
- "Did customers care about differentiation, or did they only want the cheapest option?"
- "How is profit margin different from total revenue?"
- "Connect to the story: Why did Ellis decide to RAISE prices instead of lowering them?"
- "What was Maplewood Makers' competitive advantage? (Quality, guarantees, customer relationships)"
- "Real world: Can you think of businesses that charge MORE but people still buy from them? (Apple, Starbucks, etc.) Why?"
- "What's the risk of always being the cheapest option in your market?"

**Extension Ideas:**
- Research real-world examples of differentiation (Southwest Airlines: friendly service; Volvo: safety; In-N-Out Burger: fresh ingredients)
- Create advertising campaigns highlighting each team's competitive advantage
- Write business case studies: "When [Team Name] competed on price, profit was $X. When they differentiated with [strategy], profit became $Y."
- Graph long-term scenarios: "If you make $1 profit per item vs. $4 profit per item, how many must you sell to earn $100?"
- Debate: "Should businesses ever compete primarily on price? When might that strategy make sense?"

## DIFFERENTIATION NOTES

**By Reading Level (Tier):**

**Tier 1 (Levels A-D / K-1):**
- Simple price comparison: "This costs $2, this costs $1. Which is cheaper?"
- One differentiation choice: "This one has your name on it. This one doesn't."
- Focus on observable differences, not abstract concepts
- Practice making choices between price and features
- Basic vocabulary: cheap, expensive, special, different

**Tier 2 (Levels E-J / 1st-2nd):**
- Understand that lowest price isn't always best choice
- Simple differentiation: "We're faster" or "We add decorations"
- Recognize that quality and service matter to customers
- Basic profit calculation with teacher support
- Vocabulary expansion: compete, customer, profit, special offer

**Tier 3 (Levels K-M / 2nd Grade):**
- Calculate profit: Price - Cost = Profit
- Understand profit margin concept (simple percentage)
- Brainstorm multiple differentiation strategies
- Recognize cause and effect: price wars → no profit
- Vocabulary: profit margin, competitive advantage, differentiation, quality

**Tier 4 (Levels N-O-P / 3rd-4th):**
- Calculate profit margin as percentage: (Revenue - Cost) / Revenue × 100
- Analyze data from both rounds and graph results
- Design multi-faceted differentiation strategies
- Understand long-term vs. short-term business thinking
- Advanced vocabulary: market positioning, value proposition, sustainable advantage, race to the bottom, brand loyalty

**For English Language Learners:**
- Visual price tags with numbers clearly displayed
- Bilingual vocabulary cards: competition/competencia, quality/calidad, customer/cliente, profit/ganancia
- Use gestures and visual comparisons (thumbs up for "better quality," coins for "cheaper")
- Sentence frames: "My business is special because ___." "I choose this store because ___."
- Pair with fluent speaker for strategy discussions

**For Students with Learning Differences:**
- Simplified choices (2 businesses instead of 4)
- Visual "price vs. quality" chart to support decision-making
- Step-by-step strategy worksheet with checkboxes
- Option to observe Round 1 before participating in Round 2
- Clear role assignment if overwhelmed by open competition
- Teacher support during profit calculations

**For Advanced Students:**
- Lead market analysis and data presentation
- Calculate and graph profit margins for all teams
- Research and present real-world differentiation examples
- Create "business consultant" reports advising which strategies worked best
- Design long-term scenarios: "If this continues for a year, what happens?"
- Calculate break-even points and ROI (return on investment)

## FL INTEGRATION

**Chapter 5 Financial Literacy Concepts:**

1. **Price Wars Destroy Profit**
   - Core Lesson: Competing only on price creates a "race to the bottom" where no one makes money
   - Activity Connection: Round 1 demonstrates that continual price cutting eliminates profit margin
   - Age-Appropriate: K-1 see it happen; 3-4 calculate actual profit/loss and understand the math

2. **Competitive Advantage Through Differentiation**
   - Core Lesson: Businesses survive by offering something unique that justifies higher prices
   - Activity Connection: Round 2 shows that customers will pay more for quality, service, customization, guarantees
   - Age-Appropriate: Concrete experience of choosing "special" over "cheapest"

3. **Quality as a Differentiator**
   - Core Lesson: Good work costs more but lasts longer and builds reputation
   - Activity Connection: Teams that emphasize quality, guarantees, or customization win customers willing to pay premium prices
   - Age-Appropriate: Direct parallel to story where Ellis's quality work beats Neville's cheap work

4. **Long-Term vs. Short-Term Thinking**
   - Core Lesson: Racing to the bottom wins short-term sales but destroys long-term business viability
   - Activity Connection: Data analysis shows differentiation produces sustainable profit while price wars don't
   - Age-Appropriate: Scaffolded from immediate experience (K-1) to strategic analysis (3-4)

**Non-Moralistic Approach:**
- Activity presents competition as natural market reality, not good vs. evil
- No shame language: "Charging less is bad" ❌ Instead: "Charging less than cost means you can't stay in business" ✓
- Acknowledges that different businesses compete in different ways (some on price, some on quality)
- Focuses on strategy and math, not character judgments
- Celebrates creative differentiation regardless of which team "wins"

**Connection to Season Arc:**
- Builds on CH04's customer service lesson: good relationships = competitive advantage
- Sets up CH06's hidden costs: knowing true costs prevents destructive price cutting
- Introduces strategic thinking used throughout remaining chapters
- Reinforces that business success requires more than just lowering prices

## TEACHER TIPS

**Preparation:**
- Prepare play money ahead of time (or have students create it as warm-up)
- Create simple business planning templates if using 30- or 45-min version
- Identify 4 clear "store" spaces in the classroom
- Have examples of differentiation strategies ready to share if teams get stuck
- Prepare data tracking method (whiteboard, chart paper, or handouts)

**Classroom Management:**
- Establish "market rules" before starting: respectful competition, no yelling, stay in your store area
- Use a bell or signal to start/stop shopping rounds
- Assign rotating customer roles if class is small
- Have a "market manager" role (could be teacher or advanced student) to track overall data
- Prepare for excitement and energy — this activity is naturally high-energy; channel it productively

**Common Challenges & Solutions:**

| Challenge | Solution |
|-----------|----------|
| Teams panic during price war and drop prices irrationally | Perfect teaching moment. Let it play out, then analyze the math together. Show them their profit turned negative. |
| One team doesn't understand differentiation and just keeps lowering price | Pause and give concrete examples: "What if your store gave a free gift with every purchase? Or let customers choose custom colors? Or promised to fix anything that breaks?" |
| Students only want to shop at the cheapest store even in Round 2 | Ask: "But does it come with a guarantee? Is it high quality? Can you get it in your favorite color?" Help them see trade-offs. |
| Teams copy each other's differentiation strategies | Discuss: If everyone offers the same "special" thing, it's no longer special. Encourage creativity. |
| Advanced students dominate strategy discussions | Assign specific roles: one student calculates profit, one designs signage, one greets customers, one tracks inventory. Everyone contributes. |
| Profit calculations are confusing for younger students | Simplify: "You sold for $5. It cost $3 to make. $5 - $3 = $2 profit." Use manipulatives (play coins) for visual support. |
| Teams get discouraged when they "lose" Round 1 | Reframe: "Round 1 was designed to show what NOT to do. Everyone struggled. That's the point. Now let's try a better way." |

**Time Management:**
- Round 1 often takes longer than expected as students experiment with pricing
- Set strict time limits for shopping periods (use visible timer)
- Have extension activities ready for teams that finish differentiation planning early
- If running short on time, skip Round 1 and just demonstrate price war concept, then move to Round 2
- If running long, pause after Round 1 and continue next day with Round 2

**Differentiation in Practice:**
- Pair less confident students with assertive teammates
- Provide differentiation idea cards for teams that get stuck
- Let students who struggle with math focus on creative/design roles
- Advanced students can be "market analysts" who track all data and present findings
- Students with social anxiety can have behind-the-scenes roles (calculator, sign maker) rather than customer-facing

## SAFETY NOTES

**Physical Safety:**
- Clear walkways between store stations to prevent collisions during shopping rounds
- Keep store areas organized to avoid tripping hazards
- Supervise any cutting/crafting if students are making physical products
- Ensure adequate space for all teams to operate simultaneously

**Emotional Safety:**
- Monitor competition intensity — stop if it becomes unkind or exclusionary
- Emphasize that this is a learning game, not a judgment of students' abilities
- Praise creative strategies regardless of which team "wins"
- Frame "failure" in Round 1 as intentional teaching tool, not actual failure
- Intervene if students make mean comments about competitors
- Have backup roles for students who feel anxious about competitive activities

**Fair Play:**
- Establish rule: no sabotaging other teams
- Rotate customer roles so all students experience both sides
- Ensure all teams start with equal resources
- Teacher monitors for rule-following during shopping periods

**General:**
- Supervision during all market activities
- Clear rules about handling play money and materials
- Plan for what happens if teams disagree about strategy (majority vote or teacher facilitation)
- Have quiet reflection space available for students who need a break from high-energy activity

## COMMON CHALLENGES

| Challenge | Why It Happens | Solution |
|-----------|---------------|----------|
| Teams in price war get so low they're "paying" customers to buy (negative profit) | Caught up in competition, not watching the math | Perfect teachable moment. Stop round, show the math: "You're charging $2 but it costs $4 to make. You lose $2 on every sale!" Discuss: Can a business survive this way? |
| Students only want to shop at cheapest store even when quality is clearly better | Trained by consumer culture to seek lowest price | Ask probing questions: "But if it breaks tomorrow, was it a good deal? Would you pay $1 more for something guaranteed to last?" Help them weigh trade-offs. |
| One team doesn't understand differentiation and feels stuck | Concept is abstract, especially for younger students | Provide concrete examples: "Your business could offer: (1) custom colors, (2) free gift wrapping, (3) 'If it breaks we fix it free,' (4) fastest service, (5) friendliest store. Pick one!" |
| Advanced students want to create complex multi-layered strategies while others want simple ones | Different developmental levels and interests | That's okay! Scaffold: simple teams can do "We add stickers." Advanced teams can do "Premium tier with custom design, standard tier basic version, loyalty program for repeat customers." All levels learn. |
| Teams copy competitors' differentiation strategies | Mimicry is natural, but defeats the purpose of being unique | Intervention: "If you both offer the same special thing, how is that different anymore? How can YOUR business be truly one-of-a-kind?" Encourage creativity. |
| Some customers have play money left over, others overspent | Natural outcome of shopping simulation | Use as reflection: "Why do some customers still have money? Did they budget? Were they picky? Is that good or bad?" Economics lesson about saving vs. spending. |
| Teams that "lost" Round 1 feel discouraged | Competition creates winners/losers emotionally | Reframe immediately: "Everyone struggled in Round 1. That was the goal — to show what NOT to do. You learned what every real business learns: price wars don't work. Now you're ready for the better strategy." |
| Students argue about profit calculations | Math is challenging, especially under competitive pressure | Pause market. Do sample calculation together on board. "If price is $10 and cost is $6, what's profit? $10 - $6 = $4. Now you try with your numbers." Provide calculator for Tier 1-2. |

## EXTENSIONS & VARIATIONS

**For Early Finishers:**
1. Create advertisement poster for their business's competitive advantage
2. Interview "customers" about why they made purchasing decisions
3. Calculate what would happen if price war continued for 10 rounds
4. Draw a comic strip showing price war vs. differentiation
5. Design a "business advice" guide for younger students

**For Advanced Learners:**
1. Calculate break-even point: "How many items must we sell at this price to cover costs?"
2. Research real-world price wars (airline industry, retail "Black Friday") and present findings
3. Create detailed financial projections: "If we maintain this profit margin for a year..."
4. Design marketing campaign for their differentiation strategy
5. Write persuasive essay: "Why competing on quality beats competing on price"
6. Graph profit margins across multiple scenarios (various price points and costs)
7. Analyze and present: "Which differentiation strategies had the highest ROI?"

**For Additional Days/Sessions:**
- **Day 2:** Run the simulation again with new differentiation strategies — did learning from Day 1 improve outcomes?
- **Week 2:** Invite another class as customers for more realistic market
- **Extension:** Research local businesses and identify their competitive advantages
- **Cross-Age:** Partner with older grades who act as "business consultants" advising younger students

**Seasonal Variations:**
- **Fall:** Compete selling "back to school supplies"
- **Winter:** Holiday market with seasonal products
- **Spring:** Compete with garden/outdoor products
- **Any time:** Adjust product type to match current curriculum (books for literacy week, art for art show, etc.)
