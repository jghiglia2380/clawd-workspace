# CHAPTER 1: THE INVITATION ARRIVES - ACTIVITY PLAN

**Chapter Theme:** Skills inventory and entrepreneurial thinking
**Story Connection:** Based on scenes where friends receive an invitation to a Makers' Faire and must figure out what they can create — each friend assesses their own skills and interests
**Target Age:** K-4th Grade (Ages 5-10)
**Activity Type:** Single-session (modular scheduling: daily, weekly, or any frequency)

---

## ACTIVITY TITLE
**"What Can YOU Make? Skills Inventory & Dream Product"**

---

## STORY CONNECTION

**Explicit Scene Links:**
- **Scenes 1-4:** Friends receive mysterious invitation to Makers' Faire → Activity begins with excitement about creating something
- **Scenes 5-8:** Each friend considers their individual skills and talents → Activity includes personal skills inventory
- **Scenes 9-12:** Layla realizes she's good at organizing, Riley at art, Ellis at building, Benny at ideas → Activity demonstrates different types of maker skills
- **Scenes 13-16:** Friends start brainstorming what they could actually make and sell → Activity includes dream product design
- **Scenes 17-20:** They commit to learning and making together → Activity concludes with sharing ideas and building excitement for the season

---

## LEARNING OBJECTIVES

1. **Financial Literacy:** Understand that skills have economic value and can be turned into products
2. **Self-Assessment:** Identify personal strengths and interests as foundation for entrepreneurship
3. **Product Development:** Learn that products begin as ideas based on maker capabilities
4. **Entrepreneurial Mindset:** Develop "What can I make and sell?" thinking
5. **Goal Setting:** Set learning and creation goals for the season
6. **Confidence Building:** Recognize existing skills while identifying areas for growth

---

## THREE TIME OPTIONS

### 🕐 20-MINUTE VERSION (Story + Quick Activity)
**Best for:** Tight schedules, younger students (K-1), daily scheduling
**Total time:** 10 min story + 10 min activity = 20 minutes

**Activity:** Quick Skills Checklist & One Dream Product

**Materials Needed:**
- Simple skills checklist (pre-printed or on board)
- Paper for drawing
- Pencils or crayons

**Steps:**
1. **Skills Check (3 min):** Go through checklist together. Raise hand if you can: draw, build with blocks, cook/mix, garden, sew/craft, write stories, solve puzzles, organize things, come up with ideas, make people laugh. Students call out their top 2 skills.
2. **Dream Product Draw (5 min):** Draw ONE product you wish you could make. Name it. Show at least one picture.
3. **Quick Share (2 min):** Turn to partner. Show your drawing. Say: "I want to make a ___."

**Discussion Questions:**
- "What can you already do?"
- "What would you like to make someday?"
- "Who would want to buy your product?"

---

### 🕑 30-MINUTE VERSION (Story + Standard Activity)
**Best for:** Standard class periods, grades 1-3, weekly scheduling
**Total time:** 10 min story + 20 min activity = 30 minutes

**Activity:** Skills Inventory & Dream Product Design Card

**Materials Needed:**
- Skills inventory worksheet (categories: Art, Building, Writing, Numbers, Organizing, Ideas, Helping)
- Dream product design template
- Colored pencils, markers
- Examples of maker products (photos or real items)

**Steps:**
1. **Skills Inventory (8 min):** Complete worksheet with guidance. For each category, check boxes for things you can do. Count your skills in each category. Circle your top 2 categories — these are your "maker strengths."
2. **Product Examples Tour (3 min):** Teacher shows/discusses 5-6 maker products: painted mugs, sewn pouches, built birdhouses, decorated cookies, handmade cards, organized kits. Point out which skills each uses.
3. **Dream Product Design (7 min):** On design card, draw your dream product. Include: product name, what it does/who uses it, which of YOUR skills you'd use to make it, and simple price guess.
4. **Gallery Walk & Share (2 min):** Post designs around room. Students walk around viewing others' ideas. Return to seats and share one product you'd want to buy from a classmate.

**Discussion Questions:**
- "What skills are you already good at?"
- "What skills would you like to learn this season?"
- "How can your skills become a product?"
- "What makes a good product idea?"
- "Who would be your customer?"

**Extension:** Create a class "Skills Inventory Chart" showing all students' strengths — celebrate diverse talents.

---

### 🕒 45-MINUTE VERSION (Story + Extended Activity)
**Best for:** Block schedules, grades 3-4, deeper exploration
**Total time:** 10 min story + 35 min activity = 45 minutes

**Activity:** Complete Maker's Profile & Product Pitch

**Materials Needed:**
- Detailed skills inventory (10+ categories)
- Dream product design sheet with sketch space, specifications, target customer
- Elevator pitch planning template
- Markers, colored pencils
- Timer for pitches
- Optional: sample maker products for inspiration

**Steps:**
1. **Comprehensive Skills Inventory (10 min):**
   - Complete detailed inventory across categories: Visual Arts, Construction/Building, Culinary Arts, Textile/Fabric, Writing/Storytelling, Music/Performance, Organization/Planning, Problem-Solving, Technology/Digital, Nature/Gardening
   - Rate yourself 1-5 in each (1=beginner, 5=expert)
   - Calculate your top 3 skill areas
   - Write one sentence: "I am a maker who is good at ___, ___, and ___."
2. **Product Research & Brainstorm (5 min):**
   - Review example products that use skills similar to yours
   - Brainstorm 5 possible products you could make using your skills
   - Choose the ONE you're most excited about
3. **Detailed Product Design (10 min):**
   - Sketch your product from multiple angles
   - Name it (be creative!)
   - Write description: What is it? What does it do? Who needs it?
   - Identify target customer (age, interests, why they'd buy it)
   - Estimate: Materials needed, time to make one, price you'd charge
   - Note: What skills do you already have? What would you need to learn?
4. **Elevator Pitch Practice (8 min):**
   - Using template, write 30-second pitch: "Hi, I'm [name]. I'm a maker who can [skills]. I designed [product] for people who [need]. I'm excited to make this because [reason]."
   - Practice with partner
   - 6-8 volunteers present to class
5. **Peer Feedback & Reflection (2 min):**
   - Classmates give one compliment per pitch
   - Self-reflection: "After today, I'm excited to make ___."

**Discussion Questions:**
- "How did identifying your skills help you think of product ideas?"
- "What's the difference between a skill and a product?"
- "Why do different people have different maker skills?"
- "If you don't have all the skills yet, can you still be a maker?" (YES!)
- "How do you turn an idea into something you can actually make?"
- "What makes a product worth buying?"

**Extension Ideas:**
- Create digital "Maker Profile" posters to display
- Research real makers in your community and their skills
- Start a "Skill Swap" where students teach each other (art kid teaches drawing, building kid teaches construction)
- Interview family members about their making skills
- Create season-long "Learning Goals" based on skills you want to develop

## DIFFERENTIATION NOTES

**By Reading Level (Tier):**

**Tier 1 (Levels A-D / K-1):**
- Simple verbal skills checklist with teacher guidance
- Draw one picture of something they can make (no text required)
- Focus on "I can..." statements with concrete examples
- Share with partner instead of whole class
- Basic vocabulary: make, build, draw, create

**Tier 2 (Levels E-J / 1st-2nd):**
- Picture-based skills inventory with simple words
- Product design includes name and simple sentence description
- Use sentence frame: "I can make a ___ for people who ___."
- Share in small groups with peer support
- Vocabulary expansion: skill, product, maker, customer, design

**Tier 3 (Levels K-M / 2nd Grade):**
- Full skills inventory with written responses
- Product design includes sketch, name, purpose, and customer
- Practice explaining ideas to others
- Begin thinking about "how to make it"
- Vocabulary: talent, ability, entrepreneur, target customer, prototype

**Tier 4 (Levels N-O-P / 3rd-4th):**
- Comprehensive skills assessment with self-rating
- Detailed product design with materials list and price estimate
- Formal elevator pitch with business language
- Consider learning curve: "What do I need to learn to make this real?"
- Advanced vocabulary: inventory, competency, value proposition, market research, feasibility

**For English Language Learners:**
- Visual skills inventory with pictures next to words
- Bilingual vocabulary: skills/habilidades, make/hacer, product/producto, sell/vender
- Partner with fluent speaker for pitch practice
- Allow drawing-heavy design with minimal text
- Sentence frames for pitch: "I can ___. I want to make ___. People will buy it because ___."

**For Students with Learning Differences:**
- Pre-filled skills inventory with check-box format (less writing)
- Teacher or peer scribe for product design ideas
- Visual examples of each skill category to aid understanding
- Option to present pitch to teacher only, not whole class
- Extra time for sketching and thinking

**For Advanced Students:**
- Research real makers in your skill area (artists, carpenters, chefs, etc.)
- Design 3 products and compare which is most feasible
- Create a "learning plan" for acquiring new skills needed
- Help peers develop their pitches
- Consider cost of materials and time in pricing

## FL INTEGRATION

**Chapter 1 Financial Literacy Concepts:**

1. **Skills as Economic Assets**
   - Core Lesson: Your abilities have real economic value — what you can DO can be turned into money
   - Activity Connection: Students directly connect personal skills to products they could sell
   - Age-Appropriate: K-1 see that "I can draw" → "I can sell drawings"; 3-4 understand skill development as investment

2. **Entrepreneurial Thinking**
   - Core Lesson: Businesses start by asking "What can I make/do that people need?"
   - Activity Connection: Product brainstorming based on personal capabilities
   - Age-Appropriate: Even young students can practice "what could I make?" thinking

3. **Self-Assessment in Business**
   - Core Lesson: Knowing your strengths and limitations helps you choose the right business path
   - Activity Connection: Skills inventory reveals realistic starting points
   - Age-Appropriate: Scaffolded from simple "I can/I can't" to detailed self-rating

4. **Product Development Foundation**
   - Core Lesson: Products don't appear from nowhere — they come from maker skills + ideas + effort
   - Activity Connection: Dream product must connect to actual skills student has or can learn
   - Age-Appropriate: Concrete experience of idea → design process

**Non-Moralistic Approach:**
- Activity celebrates ALL types of skills, not just academic or artistic
- No hierarchy: organizing skills = building skills = idea skills (all valuable)
- Acknowledges that everyone starts somewhere — you don't need to be an expert
- Focuses on growth mindset: "What can I learn?" not "What can't I do?"
- Celebrates diverse products and customer needs

**Connection to Season Arc:**
- Foundation for entire Season 3: identifies skills that will be developed across chapters
- Sets up woodworking (CH2-4), pottery (CH5-7), invention (CH8-9), and production (CH10-11)
- Establishes growth mindset: you're a maker NOW, and you'll learn more as season progresses
- Creates excitement for Makers' Faire finale (CH12)

## TEACHER TIPS

**Preparation:**
- Gather 5-10 example maker products before class (handmade items, crafts, student work from previous years)
- Pre-print skills inventories OR prepare to display categories on board
- Consider: will students keep their work in a Season 3 "Maker Portfolio"? If so, prepare folders.
- Review the 4 characters' strengths from the story to reference during activity

**Classroom Management:**
- Celebrate EVERY skill — avoid implying some skills are "better" than others
- For students who say "I'm not good at anything," prompt with specifics: "Can you tie shoes? Stack blocks? Make people laugh? Those are skills!"
- Have examples ready for students who struggle with product ideas
- If whole-class pitches feel intimidating, do small group shares instead
- Set positive tone: this is about discovery and excitement, not judgment

**Common Challenges & Solutions:**

| Challenge | Solution |
|-----------|----------|
| "I'm not good at anything" | Reframe: "You do lots of things every day! Can you draw a smiley face? That's art skills. Can you clean your room? That's organizing skills." |
| Student's product idea is impossible/unrealistic | Celebrate the creativity, then guide: "That's amazing! Let's think about a version you could actually make this year..." |
| Product idea has nothing to do with their skills | Ask: "What skills would you need to make that? Do you have them, or would you need to learn?" Connect to growth mindset. |
| Student copies another's product idea | "I love that you like that idea! Can you make your OWN version that's a little different?" Encourage variation. |
| Perfectionist student struggles to choose ONE idea | "All your ideas are great! For today, pick the one you're MOST excited about. You can explore others later." |
| Student wants to make something dangerous or inappropriate | Redirect to safety/appropriateness: "That's creative, but [reason]. What about a version that's safer/more appropriate?" |

**Time Management:**
- Skills inventory takes longer than expected for younger students — build in buffer
- If running short on time, skip elevator pitches and save for next session
- If running long, gallery walk can be homework (post online or take home)
- Have early finishers start sketching detailed views or brainstorming other product ideas

**Differentiation in Practice:**
- Pair struggling students with maker-confident buddies for brainstorming
- Let shy students practice pitch with you privately before sharing
- Provide visual skill examples for students who need concrete references
- Advanced students can research real makers and compare their ideas

## SAFETY NOTES

**Physical Safety:**
- No safety concerns for this paper-based, discussion-focused activity
- If showing physical product examples, ensure no sharp edges or small parts for K-1

**Emotional Safety:**
- **Celebrate ALL Skills:** Avoid implying some talents are more valuable than others
- **Growth Mindset Language:** Emphasize learning and development, not fixed abilities
- **No Comparison:** Students should focus on their own journey, not competition
- **Realistic Encouragement:** Support big dreams while gently guiding toward achievable starting points
- **Privacy:** Some students may not want to share publicly — allow pass option or share with teacher only

**Inclusivity:**
- Recognize non-academic skills (social skills, humor, kindness, physical skills)
- Avoid gendered assumptions about who can do what (anyone can build, sew, cook, etc.)
- Celebrate diverse cultural making traditions if relevant to your students
- Support students with disabilities in identifying their unique capabilities

## COMMON CHALLENGES

| Challenge | Why It Happens | Solution |
|-----------|---------------|----------|
| Student says "I can't do anything" | Low confidence; hasn't thought of everyday actions as "skills" | Prompt with specifics: "Can you color? Write your name? Stack blocks? Clean up? Those are ALL skills!" Reframe the ordinary as valuable. |
| Product idea is fantasy/impossible | Imagination unconstrained by reality; lack of experience with production | Celebrate the creativity, then ask: "What's a simpler version you could make THIS year?" Guide toward achievable prototype. |
| Student copies peer's idea exactly | Uncertainty, lack of confidence in own ideas | "I love that you like [peer]'s idea! Can you add YOUR special touch to make it unique?" Encourage personalization. |
| Can't think of ANY product ideas | Overwhelmed by blank slate; needs scaffolding | Show examples: "Here are 10 maker products. Which is closest to your skills? Could you make something similar?" Use examples as springboards. |
| Product idea unrelated to their skills | Desires don't match abilities yet | "That's cool! What skills would you need to make that? Could you LEARN those skills this season?" Turns mismatch into learning goal. |
| Perfectionist paralysis | Fear of choosing "wrong" idea | "There's no wrong answer! Pick the one that makes you most excited TODAY. You can always change your mind or make multiple things." Remove pressure. |
| Too many ideas, can't choose | Creative abundance | "You're full of great ideas! For today, which ONE are you most excited to talk about? Save the others for later chapters." |

## EXTENSIONS & VARIATIONS

**For Early Finishers:**
1. Sketch product from multiple angles (front, side, top)
2. Design packaging or logo for their product
3. Interview classmate about their skills and help them brainstorm products
4. Research one real maker whose skills match theirs
5. Create "advertisement poster" for their dream product

**For Advanced Learners:**
1. Design 3 different products and compare feasibility of each
2. Research the skills needed for their dream product and create learning plan
3. Calculate estimated cost to make their product
4. Create detailed blueprint with materials list
5. Write full business plan for their product
6. Film 1-minute "Shark Tank" pitch for their product

**For Additional Days/Sessions:**
- **Day 2:** Skill Swap — students teach each other one skill
- **Week 2:** Begin building a simple prototype of dream product
- **Extension:** Visit local maker space, craft fair, or artisan studio
- **Cross-Age:** Older students mentor younger on product development

**Seasonal Variations:**
- **Fall:** Focus on harvest/food products (jams, baked goods, preserves)
- **Winter:** Holiday craft products (ornaments, cards, gifts)
- **Spring:** Garden-related products (planters, seed packets, garden art)
- **Summer:** Outdoor products (painted rocks, birdhouses, sun catchers)

## CHARACTER DEVELOPMENT ALIGNMENT

**Layla (Leader/Organizer):**
- Story shows Layla realizing her strength is organizing people and projects → Activity helps students identify organizing as a valid, valuable skill
- Students can embody Layla's confidence in non-artistic skills
- Reinforces that leadership and planning are maker skills too

**Riley (Creative/Emotional):**
- Story shows Riley nervous about her artistic skills being "good enough" → Activity celebrates ALL skill levels and growth mindset
- Students experience Riley's journey from doubt to excitement
- Celebrates artistic skills while normalizing learning curves

**Ellis (Analytical/Organized):**
- Story shows Ellis methodically assessing his building skills → Activity includes structured self-assessment
- Students use Ellis's systematic approach to inventory
- Reinforces planning and analysis as part of making

**Benny (Enthusiastic/Social):**
- Story shows Benny bursting with ideas but unsure of execution → Activity validates idea generation as a critical maker skill
- Students feel Benny's excitement about possibilities
- Celebrates that "ideas people" are essential to making
