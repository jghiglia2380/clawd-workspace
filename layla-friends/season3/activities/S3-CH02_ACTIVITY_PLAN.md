# CHAPTER 2: JOURNEY TO THE WOODWORKER'S GROVE - ACTIVITY PLAN

**Chapter Theme:** Material exploration and properties
**Story Connection:** Based on scenes where friends visit a woodworker's grove and learn about different types of wood, materials, and how raw materials become useful products
**Target Age:** K-4th Grade (Ages 5-10)
**Activity Type:** Single-session (modular scheduling: daily, weekly, or any frequency)

---

## ACTIVITY TITLE
**"Wood & Materials Explorer Lab"**

---

## STORY CONNECTION

**Explicit Scene Links:**
- **Scenes 1-5:** Friends journey to the woodworker's grove and see trees becoming lumber → Activity begins with examining raw materials
- **Scenes 6-10:** They learn about different types of wood (hard vs. soft, grain patterns) → Activity includes testing material properties
- **Scenes 11-15:** Woodworker explains how material choice affects the final product → Activity demonstrates how properties determine uses
- **Scenes 16-20:** Friends touch, smell, and examine different materials → Activity provides hands-on sensory exploration

---

## LEARNING OBJECTIVES

1. **Financial Literacy:** Understand that raw materials have different costs and properties affecting product value
2. **Material Science:** Learn to identify and test properties (hardness, flexibility, weight, texture)
3. **Production Foundation:** Grasp that material choice is the first step in making
4. **Critical Thinking:** Match materials to appropriate uses based on properties
5. **Cost Awareness:** Recognize that natural vs. manufactured materials have different price points
6. **Quality Understanding:** Learn that material quality affects final product quality and price

---

## THREE TIME OPTIONS

### 🕐 20-MINUTE VERSION (Story + Quick Activity)
**Best for:** Tight schedules, younger students (K-1), daily scheduling
**Total time:** 10 min story + 10 min activity = 20 minutes

**Activity:** Material Pass-Around & Sort

**Materials Needed:**
- 5-6 different materials (wood sample, cardboard, fabric scrap, plastic piece, metal spoon, foam)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for sorting

**Steps:**
1. **Material Introduction (3 min):** Pass around each material. Students touch and observe. Name each one together.
2. **Property Call-Out (4 min):** For each material, class calls out: "Is it hard or soft? Heavy or light? Smooth or rough?"
3. **Simple Sort (2 min):** Draw two columns: "Hard" and "Soft." Students help sort materials.
4. **Quick Discussion (1 min):** "Which would you use to build a shelf? Why?"

**Discussion Questions:**
- "Which material feels the hardest?"
- "Which is the lightest?"
- "What could you make with wood?"

---

### 🕑 30-MINUTE VERSION (Story + Standard Activity)
**Best for:** Standard class periods, grades 1-3, weekly scheduling
**Total time:** 10 min story + 20 min activity = 30 minutes

**Activity:** Materials Testing Stations

**Materials Needed:**
- 6-8 material samples (wood types, cardboard, fabric, clay, metal, plastic, paper, foam)
- Testing tools (ruler for scratch test, weights for strength test, water dropper)
- Recording sheet with properties checklist
- Sorting mat with categories

**Steps:**
1. **Station Setup (2 min):** Students group into teams. Each team gets a set of materials and testing sheet.
2. **Property Testing (12 min):** Test each material: Can it bend? Can you scratch it? Does it absorb water? Is it heavy or light? Record observations on sheet.
3. **Sorting Challenge (4 min):** Sort materials into categories: "Best for Building," "Best for Art," "Best for Clothing."
4. **Share & Discuss (2 min):** Teams share their sorting choices. "Why did you put fabric in 'clothing' but not 'building'?"

**Discussion Questions:**
- "Which material was strongest? How do you know?"
- "Why wouldn't you build a house out of fabric?"
- "What makes a good building material?"
- "Which materials come from nature? Which are made in factories?"

**Extension:** Create a "Material Museum" display with labels describing each material's properties and uses.

---

### 🕒 45-MINUTE VERSION (Story + Extended Activity)
**Best for:** Block schedules, grades 3-4, deeper exploration
**Total time:** 10 min story + 35 min activity = 45 minutes

**Activity:** Complete Materials Lab with Cost Analysis

**Materials Needed:**
- 8-10 diverse materials
- Testing protocols (scratch test, bend test, weight comparison, water resistance, texture analysis)
- Data recording sheets
- Material cost chart (showing relative prices)
- Design challenge cards

**Steps:**
1. **Scientific Testing (15 min):**
   - Students work in lab groups
   - Conduct 5 standard tests on each material: scratch test (hardness), bend test (flexibility), weight test, water test, texture analysis
   - Record data systematically on lab sheets
   - Rate each material 1-5 for each property
2. **Cost Comparison (5 min):**
   - Review material cost chart (cardboard = $1, wood = $5, metal = $10, etc.)
   - Discuss: "Why does metal cost more than cardboard?"
   - Connect properties to cost: stronger/more durable = higher cost
3. **Design Challenge (10 min):**
   - Teams receive challenge card: "Design a product using only materials that cost under $5 total"
   - Must choose materials based on: what they're making, required properties, budget constraint
   - Sketch product and label which materials they'd use and why
4. **Presentations & Debrief (5 min):**
   - 3-4 teams present their designs
   - Class discusses: Did budget limit force creative choices? How did material properties guide decisions?

**Discussion Questions:**
- "How do material properties affect what you can make?"
- "Why do some materials cost more than others?"
- "If you could only buy ONE material for $10, which would you choose? Why?"
- "How does a maker decide which material to use?"
- "What's the difference between natural and manufactured materials?"
- "Can an expensive material be used badly? Can a cheap material make something valuable?"

**Extension Ideas:**
- Research where materials come from (forest → lumber, oil → plastic)
- Visit hardware store to price real materials
- Create material sample library for classroom
- Test additional properties (conductivity, biodegradability)
- Design ideal material for specific product

## TEACHER TIPS

**Preparation:**
- Collect materials at least a week in advance (ask hardware stores, construction sites, craft stores for donations/scraps)
- Test your own materials first to ensure clear property differences
- Pre-cut materials to safe, manageable sizes (no splinters, sharp edges)
- Prepare testing stations if doing rotations
- Have extras in case materials get broken/lost

**Classroom Management:**
- Establish "scientist behavior" rules: careful handling, systematic testing, honest recording
- Demonstrate each test before students begin
- Circulate to ensure safe use of materials and tools
- Have wet wipes/towels for cleanup (some materials are messy)
- Set clear time limits for each testing phase

**Common Challenges & Solutions:**

| Challenge | Solution |
|-----------|----------|
| Materials too similar (students can't tell difference) | Choose materials with very obvious property differences for younger students; subtlety for older |
| Students rush through testing | Frame as "scientist" work; remind that careful observation is the goal, not speed |
| "Which material is THE BEST?" | Reframe: "Best for WHAT? Different materials are best for different jobs." Teach context-dependent thinking. |
| Disagreement about test results | Excellent! Discuss variation in testing, need for multiple trials, subjective vs. objective properties |
| Waste/destruction of materials | Set expectation: materials are for testing, not playing. Non-destructive tests only unless specified. |
| Cost chart seems complicated | Simplify: use relative costs (stars or $ signs) instead of actual prices for younger students |

**Time Management:**
- Testing always takes longer than you think - build in buffer
- For 20-min version, teacher demos tests and students observe/respond
- If running long, have students test fewer materials or fewer properties
- Early finishers can sketch products they'd make with each material

**Differentiation in Practice:**
- Pre-pair students: pairing strong readers with hands-on learners works well in material testing
- Provide pre-filled data sheets with checkboxes for students who struggle with writing
- Let advanced students design their own tests
- Allow verbal explanations instead of written for students who struggle with writing

## SAFETY NOTES

**Physical Safety:**
- **Sharp Edges:** Pre-check all wood for splinters; sand down if needed
- **Small Parts:** For K-1, ensure materials are not choking hazards
- **Heavy Items:** If using metal or stone, ensure weights are manageable
- **Supervision:** Adult present during all testing activities
- **Tool Safety:** If using testing tools like hammers or nails, strict supervision and safety protocols

**Material Safety:**
- **Non-Toxic Only:** All materials should be non-toxic and safe to handle
- **Allergies:** Check for latex (rubber), wool, or other common allergens
- **Splinters:** Handle wood carefully; provide tweezers for splinter removal
- **Cleanup:** Wash hands after handling materials, especially if dusty

**Environmental:**
- Discuss proper disposal of materials (recycling vs. trash)
- If materials are reusable, store properly for future use

## COMMON CHALLENGES

| Challenge | Why It Happens | Solution |
|-----------|---------------|----------|
| "This test didn't work!" | Materials don't always behave predictably; testing conditions vary | "That's real science! Sometimes tests give unexpected results. What might have caused that? Try again." Embrace iteration. |
| All materials seem the same | Materials chosen are too similar; students lack experience differentiating | Use VERY different materials for beginners (foam vs. rock). Increase subtlety for advanced students. |
| "Which material is THE BEST?" | Students want one right answer; haven't grasped context-dependent thinking | "Best for WHAT? Wood is best for furniture, but fabric is best for clothes. Context matters!" Teach situation-dependent evaluation. |
| Wasteful material use | Excitement leads to carelessness | Frame as scientists/makers: "We respect our materials because they cost money and took energy to make. Test carefully." Set clear material limits. |
| Can't explain property-price connection | Abstract concept | Use concrete examples: "Metal is harder to find and process than cardboard. More work = higher cost." Connect effort to price. |
| Frustration with systematic testing | Want to explore freely; structure feels limiting | Balance: "First we test systematically so we have good data. THEN you can explore freely for 5 minutes." Structure first, freedom second. |
