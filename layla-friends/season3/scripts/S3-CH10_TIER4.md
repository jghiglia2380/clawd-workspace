## THE MAKER'S MARKET RESEARCH

### Scene 1 (100 words)
The friends gathered at their secret fort with exciting news. "There's going to be a community Maker's Market next month in the park!" Layla announced, holding up a colorful flyer. "We can make things using our new woodworking, pottery, and design skills to sell there!" Everyone loved this idea right away. They could finally use everything they'd learned from Master Thomas, the Potter, and the Gearsmith to create something useful for their community! This was the perfect chance to put their skills to work and earn some money. The thought of combining learning with business excited them.

### Scene 2 (100 words)
Benny jumped up with his usual energy. "I'll make more scarf clips for everyone--with better spacing!" he said confidently, remembering his blueprint. Riley wanted to make waterproof covers for sketchbooks and notebooks. Ellis wanted to make sturdy carrying trays with proper supports underneath. Layla wanted to make retractable pencil holders with the right string length. They had so many ideas! Their new skills could make all sorts of useful products for people to buy at fair prices. The possibilities seemed endless, limited only by their time and materials.

### Scene 3 (100 words)
"Hold on," said Ellis thoughtfully, raising his hand. He looked at their blueprints spread across the table. "How do we know what people actually want to buy?" That was a really good question that made everyone stop and think. What if they made things nobody needed or wanted? That would waste their materials and time completely. They could end up with products sitting unsold while other sellers had customers lined up. Making stuff was fun, but making the wrong stuff would be frustrating and a waste of money. They needed to find out what people wanted first.

### Scene 4 (100 words)
Layla thought carefully about this problem, remembering something her mom had said about business planning. "We should ask people first before we make anything," she suggested. "We can survey people to learn what they actually need. Then we make those things!" Ellis nodded at this logical approach. "That's really smart. We should do real market research before building anything. Businesses do research before creating products to avoid expensive mistakes." This made perfect sense to everyone. Research would lower their risk by checking whether people actually wanted their products before they spent time and money making them.

### Scene 5 (100 words)
"What exactly is market research?" asked Benny, wanting to understand. He hadn't heard the term used this way before. "Market research means finding out what customers want, need, and will pay for," Layla explained clearly. "We ask questions to lots of people. We learn what they really want. Then we know what to make with confidence. Research prevents mistakes and wasted effort by giving us real information about demand." This was like how they'd learned to plan with blueprints before building--research was planning for business decisions. Just as blueprints prevented building mistakes, research prevented business mistakes.

### Scene 6 (100 words)
Riley got paper and pencils from their supplies. "We can write a customer survey to collect information," she said, remembering surveys from school science projects. "A survey is a list of questions you ask many people to get answers you can compare." The friends worked together at the table. They wrote thoughtful questions about what people needed at home, at work, or at school. Good surveys help you gather useful information from many people without bias. How you word the questions matters a lot--neutral questions get honest answers, while leading questions mess up your data.

### Scene 7 (100 words)
They wrote their first question carefully with attention to being clear. "What do you need help organizing at home or work?" was one important question that could reveal real needs. "What daily problems do you have that a product could solve?" was another useful one. "What bugs you about current solutions?" added a third angle. Ellis wrote ten well-thought-out questions on their survey form with proper spacing. Their survey looked pretty professional with clear formatting and logical flow! Good questions get helpful answers you can actually use. They also asked about age and household size to spot patterns among groups.

### Scene 8 (100 words)
The next question was really important for business. "How much would you pay for this item?" asked Riley, thinking about pricing. "We need to know what people are willing to spend so we don't charge too much or too little." Layla agreed completely. "If we charge too much, nobody buys it. If we charge too little, we lose money. Price matters a lot." They also needed to know their costs to make sure they'd earn a profit. Research showed what price the market would accept, but their costs set the minimum they could charge and still break even.

### Scene 9 (100 words)
The friends made twenty copies of their survey at the library. They each got five copies to fill out in different areas. "Let's go ask our neighbors," said Layla with determination. The friends walked through their neighborhood together with clipboards and pens. This was their first real market research project! They were collecting their own original data about what potential customers needed. Collecting your own data is called primary research--it's more work than looking up someone else's findings, but it's more relevant and specific to what you're doing.

### Scene 10 (100 words)
At the first house, Layla knocked politely and waited. Mrs. Chen answered with a friendly smile. "We're doing research for a community Maker's Market next month," Layla explained clearly. "Can we ask you some questions about what you need? It'll just take five minutes." Mrs. Chen smiled and opened the door wider. "Of course! I love that you're asking what people need instead of just making random stuff. That's smart thinking."

### Scene 11 (100 words)
Mrs. Chen looked at the survey questions carefully, taking her time. "I really need better hooks for my keys that match my decor," she said, naming a clear need. She also needed a holder for her reading glasses that she kept losing around the house. Layla wrote down both answers neatly with details. This information was really helpful for planning what to make! Real customer feedback showed needs they wouldn't have guessed on their own. Knowing what people actually need helps you design better solutions. Mrs. Chen added she'd pay five to eight dollars for key hooks and three to five for a glasses holder.

### Scene 12 (100 words)
At the next house, Mr. Rodriguez had completely different needs. "I really need something to organize my mail," he explained with obvious frustration. "It gets so messy on my entry table every day--bills mix with magazines, important letters get buried. I need compartments." Ellis wrote this down carefully. People had so many different needs in their homes depending on their lifestyle and family! Every household was different with different problems needing different solutions. Variety in needs meant they'd need variety in products. Mr. Rodriguez would pay ten to fifteen dollars for a good mail organizer--more than key hooks because it solved a bigger daily problem.

### Scene 13 (100 words)
They surveyed twenty families through the afternoon. Visiting homes on three different streets for variety. Each family had different needs based on their situation and lifestyle. Some wanted organizers for various things--tools, craft supplies, paperwork. Some wanted plant holders for gardens and windowsills. Some wanted hooks for bags, coats, and accessories. The friends wrote everything down carefully in organized notes. They were learning what people really wanted and would actually buy! The range of answers was interesting and showed that no single product would work for everyone. A good market stall would need variety to. Appeal to different customers with different needs and budgets.

### Scene 14 (100 words)
Back at the fort, they looked at all the survey answers together, spreading out their notes. "Check out this pattern!" said Ellis excitedly, pointing at the data on a summary sheet. "Eight people want key hooks--that's forty percent! That's the most popular need by far." Patterns were clearly showing up in the data they'd collected. Patterns help you understand what customers want most and where demand is strongest. Data reveals demand that might not be obvious just from guessing. They made a chart showing how often each need was mentioned, which made the priorities obvious. Key hooks led, followed by plant holders and mail organizers.

### Scene 15 (100 words)
Riley counted and sorted all the answers using tally marks and percentages. "Five people want plant holders--that's twenty-five percent," she reported from her count. "Three people want mail organizers--fifteen percent." They made a ranked list by how often things came up. The list showed what was needed most in their community with clear priorities. This data helped them decide wisely what to make first with their limited time and materials. Data drives smart business decisions by showing where to put your energy. They would focus on high-demand items first. Making what people wanted most gave them the best shot at selling well at the market.

### Scene 16 (100 words)
"This research is so helpful!" said Benny with real appreciation. "Before we asked people, I was going to make scarf clips because I already had a blueprint. But nobody even asked for those in our survey!" Layla nodded, seeing the lesson clearly. "We could've wasted our time and materials on stuff nobody wanted. Research helps us make smart choices and avoid big mistakes. It saves money and resources." The research had already paid off by stopping them from making products with zero demand. This was validation--testing demand before production. Companies that skip this step often fail because they build things nobody wants.

### Scene 17 (100 words)
Ellis explained the economics more clearly. "If we make what people want, we'll sell more and earn more from our time," he reasoned. "If we make what people don't want, we waste materials and time with zero sales. Research saves us money and time! It also cuts waste because we make only what people will actually buy." This was the basic economic idea of matching supply to demand. Making too much creates waste. Making too little means lost sales. Research helped them find the right amount of each product to make.

### Scene 18 (100 words)
The friends decided together based on their research. They would make key hooks first. Because eight people wanted them--that was strong. Proven demand. Then plant holders because five people asked for those--solid demand worth serving. They would use their woodworking and design skills to meet real community needs without waste. Supply should match demand to avoid waste and boost success. Understanding demand helps you use your resources wisely. They would make twelve key hooks and eight plant holders. Slightly more than surveyed demand to cover extra customers they hadn't surveyed. This was production planning based on research--exactly how real businesses work.

### Scene 19 (100 words)
"We asked before we made anything," said Riley proudly. "That's smart business." Layla agreed. "We learned exactly what our community needs right now. Now we can help them with useful products that solve real problems. That feels really good! This is how good markets work. Businesses research customer needs before making products to make sure demand exists and resources aren't wasted." This was customer-focused thinking instead of product-focused thinking. Instead of making what they wanted to make, they were making what customers needed. This approach separated successful businesses from failures.

### Scene 20 (100 words)
The friends started drawing blueprints for key hooks and plant holders right away, energized by their clear direction. They knew exactly what to make and why, based on solid data. Market research had saved them from making the wrong things and wasting their limited resources on unsold stuff. They felt prepared and confident about the Maker's Market, knowing their products would find buyers. "Let's make exactly what people need!" said Layla with determination. Research made them smart, efficient makers serving real needs rather than guessing and hoping. They were ready to succeed because they'd done the homework first.

## FL VOCABULARY

- **market research** -- Finding out what people want, need, and will pay for before making something to sell
- **survey** -- A list of questions. You ask many people to gather information and learn about their needs
- **customer needs** -- The things people want or need to buy to solve problems or improve their lives
- **demand** -- How much people want to buy something. Strong demand means many people want it
- **supply** -- The amount of something that is. Made or available to sell at a given price
- **primary research** -- Collecting your own original data directly from people
- **market validation** -- Testing whether demand. Exists for a product before making it
- **resource allocation** -- Deciding how to spread limited time, money, and materials across priorities based on data
